A written response can contain sophisticated French and still miss its purpose. Useful feedback explains both what the reader needs and what the writer needs to improve.

> **Do not reduce writing to one criterion. Task fulfilment, organization and language all matter; their official weighting depends on the examination.**

## Begin with the actual instruction

Before drafting, identify who is writing, who will read the text and what the text must achieve. A description, a personal message and an argument require different choices. A memorized essay can be fluent yet inappropriate for a short practical task.

Check the required length for that specific task. There is no universal “250 words” rule for French tests. For example, TEF Canada and TCF Canada use different task structures and length instructions.

## Use four questions when reviewing a draft

| Area | Review question |
|---|---|
| Task | Have I included the requested information and purpose? |
| Organization | Does each paragraph develop a clear idea? |
| Vocabulary | Are my words precise, appropriate and correctly combined? |
| Accuracy | Do grammar and spelling support clear meaning? |

This is a practical review framework, not an official scoring formula. Avoid assigning invented percentages or assuming that connectors matter more than every other feature.

## Develop an idea before decorating it

“Public transport is useful” becomes more convincing when followed by a reason and a concrete consequence. Adding five connectors without adding content does not create an argument. Likewise, replacing a simple word with an unfamiliar synonym can make a sentence less accurate.

Plan briefly, draft a complete response, then review in separate passes. First check purpose and missing content. Next check paragraph order. Finally check recurring language issues such as verb tense or agreement.

Keep the original and revised versions together. Write one sentence explaining what changed and why. This makes feedback reusable on the next topic instead of turning correction into a page of marks you never revisit.
