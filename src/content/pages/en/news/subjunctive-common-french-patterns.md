The subjunctive is often introduced as the mood of uncertainty. That is a useful hint, but it does not explain every case. French also uses it after expressions of necessity, wishes and emotion—even when the underlying event is real.

> **Learn complete constructions. The word *que* alone does not trigger the subjunctive, and “subjective versus factual” is not a sufficient rule.**

## Start with frequent constructions

| Construction | Example |
|---|---|
| Necessity | *Il faut que tu viennes.* |
| Wish | *Je veux qu’il comprenne.* |
| Emotion | *Je suis contente que tu sois là.* |
| Concession | *Bien qu’il pleuve, nous sortons.* |
| Before an event | *Je t’appellerai avant que tu partes.* |

In the emotion example, the person really is present. The subjunctive follows the construction, not a claim that their presence is uncertain.

## Notice the contrasts

An affirmative *je pense que* generally takes the indicative: *Je pense qu’il vient*. A negative or interrogative construction can involve different choices depending on meaning; it should not be reduced to a rule that every negative sentence automatically takes the subjunctive.

In standard usage, *après que* takes the indicative, unlike *avant que*. Affirmative *espérer que* generally takes the indicative too: *J’espère qu’il viendra*. These contrasts are good reasons to learn the whole expression rather than responding mechanically to *que*.

## Avoid unnecessary subordinate clauses

When the subject stays the same, an infinitive is often the natural choice: *Je veux partir* rather than forcing a *que* clause. Compare it with *Je veux que tu partes*, where the subject changes.

## Practise one meaningful change at a time

Take a familiar sentence and change the construction: a statement becomes a wish, then a necessary action. Check both the verb form and the intended relationship between the ideas.

Keep a small set of reliable examples, including common irregular forms such as *sois*, *aie*, *fasse* and *puisse*. Add exceptions and more nuanced uses gradually. Accurate, appropriate constructions communicate more effectively than inserting the subjunctive simply to make a text look advanced.
