A lower CRS cut-off attracts attention. The tie-breaking date printed beside it can look like a second signal about the candidate pool. It has a much narrower purpose.

> **The tie-break applies to candidates tied at the lowest invited CRS score. Among those candidates, earlier profile submission takes precedence.**

## Work through a simple example

Imagine a round with a cut-off of 400 and a tie-breaking timestamp of June 1 at noon. Candidates above 400 who satisfy that round’s criteria are ranked ahead of the cut-off group. Within the group at 400, the relevant profile-submission timestamp determines which tied candidates receive invitations.

A candidate at 400 with a qualifying timestamp before the stated limit can be included. Another at 400 with a later timestamp can miss that round. This does not mean recent profiles receive preference, and it does not create a general expiry date for older profiles.

## Why a changing timestamp is not a pool census

A tie-break describes a boundary within a particular score group on a particular day. It does not count all French-speaking candidates, identify everyone remaining below the cut-off, or reveal how many new profiles will enter next week.

Similarly, IRCC’s overall CRS distribution describes the broader Express Entry pool. It cannot automatically be treated as a distribution of the French-language subset.

| Observation | Safe interpretation |
|---|---|
| The cut-off fell | The lowest invited score was lower in that round. |
| The tie-break date moved | The boundary among tied candidates changed. |
| More invitations were issued | That round selected a larger number of candidates. |

## Plan without turning a result into a forecast

Round size, elapsed time and the mix of eligible profiles can all affect a cut-off. Public results alone do not isolate the contribution of each factor. A claim that “the pool is thinning” therefore needs additional evidence.

For your own profile, keep information accurate, maintain valid test results and check the rules for the round concerned. Use the tie-break to interpret a published result—not to estimate when an invitation must arrive.
