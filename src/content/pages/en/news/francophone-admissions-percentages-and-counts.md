A percentage can rise while the number of people it describes falls. The 2025 Francophone admissions figures illustrate why a headline percentage needs both a population and a comparison year.

> **Compare like with like:** 8.5% was the 2025 target. The 8.9% result exceeded that target. To measure change over time, compare actual results for 2024 and 2025.

## Put the four numbers side by side

IRCC’s updated dashboard uses the same geographic scope—outside Quebec—for the following results:

| Year | French-speaking permanent residents admitted | Share of admissions outside Quebec |
|---|---:|---:|
| 2024 | 30,565 | 7.2% |
| 2025 | 29,630 | 8.9% |

The count fell by 935, about 3.1%, while the share increased by 1.7 percentage points. These statements are compatible. The denominator—the total number of permanent residents admitted outside Quebec—also changed.

## Why the national plan is not the right denominator

Dividing a Francophone count outside Quebec by a national planning target mixes geography and measurement. A plan is not an actual admissions total, and national admissions include Quebec. That shortcut may produce a neat explanation, but it does not establish what happened in the dashboard.

Even reconstructing the denominator from the published percentages gives only an approximation because those percentages are rounded. For an exact total, consult the underlying admissions dataset rather than treating a rounded rate as an exact fraction.

## What the result can and cannot explain

The figures show an increased Francophone share within a smaller admissions context. They do not establish an individual’s probability of selection, the size of the French-language Express Entry pool, or the number of future invitations.

When comparing news reports, use a simple sequence: identify the population, distinguish target from outcome, compare the same measure across years, then consider the interpretation. A percentage is useful evidence once those checks are complete. On its own, it can tell an incomplete story.
