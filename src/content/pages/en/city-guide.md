## Who this guide is for

Three kinds of readers:

- You have just arrived in Toronto and want to put your French to use.
- Your child is in a French school or French Immersion class and you are struggling to keep up.
- You plan to stay long term and want to join the francophone community or reach bilingual jobs.

## Children's French education: two paths, don't mix them up

This is what parents confuse most often, so let's clear it up first.

| | French-language school | French Immersion class |
|---|---|---|
| Run by | A French-language school board (for example Viamonde, MonAvenir) | An English-language school board (for example TDSB) |
| Language of instruction | **Entirely in French** | about half French, half English |
| Who can enrol | Usually one parent must be a rights holder (*ayant droit*), meaning entitled to French-language education under section 23 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms | All students, by registration and school catchment area |
| Starting point | From kindergarten | Mainly early immersion (JK) or middle immersion (Grade 4) |
| Intensity | High | Medium |

**The difference in one sentence**: a French-language school is a separate school system from the English boards, entirely in French; an immersion class is held in an English school, where part of the instruction is in French.

Both are good choices, but they suit different children. Mixing them up can cost you a year.

## The three questions parents ask most

**"My child can't keep up in immersion. What can I do?"**
First work out whether the problem is speaking and listening or reading and writing. For most children the bottleneck is in **reading and writing**, not speaking and listening: everyday conversation is fine, but spelling, grammar and composition clearly lag. "Speaking more French" won't close that gap; it takes training aimed at reading and writing.

**"No one at home speaks French. Can we still support our child?"**
Yes, within limits. Parents can make sure there is enough exposure (French cartoons, audiobooks, a fixed time each week) and good attendance. They can't check grammar and compositions; leave that to the teacher. **The worst thing is parental anxiety, which makes the child resist.**

**"When is the right time to start?"**
The entry points for immersion are set in advance; just register according to your school catchment area. Extra support outside school can begin at any time, but we suggest waiting until your child shows signs of falling behind rather than adding extra work early.

## Adults: ways to learn French in Toronto

| Route | Best for | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Language school (like ours) | A clear goal (exam, job) | Levels, progress tracking, mock exams |
| Community centres and libraries | Limited budget, keeping your ear in | Inexpensive, flexible hours, no structured progression |
| Cultural institution courses | Mostly personal interest | Good cultural atmosphere, little exam focus |
| University continuing education | Need a certificate and have plenty of time | Long programs, more academic |

## Where the francophone community is

Toronto's francophone community isn't concentrated the way Montréal's is, but the organizations are all here.

| Organization | What it does |
|---|---|
| Centre francophone du Grand Toronto (CFGT) | Newcomer settlement, employment, social services |
| Alliance Française Toronto | French courses and cultural events |
| Théâtre français de Toronto | French-language theatre |
| French school parent councils | Community events, parent network |
| Local French-language churches and associations | Depending on the neighbourhood |

**How to find events**: check the community calendars of the French-language media (next section). It works better than searching social media at random.

## French-language media: free listening resources

| Media | Type | For learners |
|---|---|---|
| Radio-Canada (ICI Première Ontario) | Radio | Local news, medium pace |
| TFO | Ontario French-language television | **Children's programs are best for beginners**: slow pace, simple vocabulary |
| L'Express | Toronto's French-language weekly | Local news and community information, short articles |
| Radio-Canada OHdio | Podcast platform | Practise listening on your commute |

**Advice for beginners**: start with TFO's children's programs. Don't jump straight into the news: the pace is fast and there are many proper names, and not understanding anything can hurt your confidence.

## Can you handle daily errands in French?

| Situation | What to expect |
|---|---|
| Federal services (immigration, taxes, passports) | Bilingual service is required by law; you can ask for French |
| Ontario services (ServiceOntario) | Some counters offer French; best to confirm in advance |
| Health care | Some hospitals have a French-language services coordinator; you can ask before your appointment |
| Emergency, 911 | You can ask for a French-speaking operator |
| Banks and insurance | National institutions often have a bilingual line |

**Practical tip**: before calling, say "Bonjour, est-ce que je peux être servi en français?" If the service isn't available, you will be transferred, and no one will mind.

## French-language job opportunities

| Field | Situation |
|---|---|
| Federal public service | Many positions are "bilingual imperative": without the required level, you can't take the job |
| School boards | French-language schools have been short of teachers for a long time; bilingualism is a condition of hiring |
| Customer service and finance | National companies often run a bilingual line |
| Health care and social services | Positions serving francophones require French |
| Translation and localization | French is part of the ability to practise |

**Keep in mind**: these jobs want **French you can work in**, not an exam score. CLB 7 / NCLC 7 is the entry threshold; once you are in, you still need to keep practising how you express yourself.
